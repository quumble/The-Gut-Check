# Analysis Lens Policy — DRAFT

**Status:** draft house policy, pending review and explicit adoption.

## Purpose

Sequential research changes the analyst. Once a result has been seen, a construct has been named, or a hypothesis has been formed, later analyses do not become naive again simply because they are preregistered or performed in a new conversation.

The purpose of this policy is to preserve the provenance of interpretation: not only what the data show, but what the analyst already knew, expected, and was carrying into the first inspection of those data.

This is not a claim that memory, prior knowledge, or an ongoing research context invalidates an analysis. It is a requirement to name that context rather than silently treating an informed perspective as a blank one.

## Hypothesis provenance

Every substantive prediction or hypothesis should be described using an explicit provenance label when practical:

- **naive** — committed before the analyst had seen the relevant phenomenon or materially similar results;
- **prior-informed** — prospectively committed, but motivated or shaped by earlier observations, related experiments, theory, or known results;
- **replication** — a prospective test of a previously observed result, construct, or pattern;
- **exploratory / post hoc** — formulated after inspecting the data to which the claim refers.

A hypothesis may become prospective in a later test without becoming naive. Provenance does not reset between experiments.

No result should be retrospectively promoted to a stronger epistemic status than it had when the relevant data were first inspected.

## Analysis lens at first inspection

For each consequential analysis, preserve a short record of the lens through which the data were first viewed. The record should state, as applicable:

1. **Prior results known** — relevant earlier experiments or findings already available to the analyst.
2. **Existing hypotheses and constructs** — categories, mechanisms, coding concepts, or expectations already formed.
3. **Prior exposure to related outputs** — whether the analyst had already seen similar responses, subsets, pilot data, or earlier passes over the same material.
4. **Context state** — whether the analysis occurred in an ongoing context with memory/history available, a fresh temporary context, or another materially different information state.
5. **What was genuinely naive** — if anything important remained genuinely unanticipated at the point of first inspection.
6. **Analyst identity / role when relevant** — human, model, or collaborative analyst, especially where prior exposure differs between collaborators.

The goal is not to inventory every trivial fact in context. Record information that could reasonably shape what the analyst notices, predicts, codes, or treats as surprising.

## Memory and temporary contexts

An analysis performed with conversation memory or substantial prior context available should not be described as a cold or naive read when that prior context is relevant.

A temporary or fresh context may be useful as a deliberately colder comparison, but it is not required for every experiment and should not be treated as a ritual source of methodological purity. If used, its information state should be described accurately.

Likewise, an analyst who has already seen the data cannot become genuinely blind to them by hiding condition labels later. A later blinded or condition-blinded pass may still reduce some forms of bias, but prior exposure should remain disclosed.

## Preserve the genealogy

When a line of work develops sequentially, preserve the actual genealogy of the idea. A typical sequence may look like:

**surprise → exploratory interpretation → confound check → frozen construct → prospective replication → cross-family or external test**

Not every project will follow that sequence, but the historical order should remain visible. Later methodological improvements should not be projected backward onto earlier work.

## Deviations and perspective changes

If the analytic lens changes after first inspection — for example, a new category becomes visible, a coding rule proves inadequate, or a prior interpretation is abandoned — preserve both the earlier lens and the change.

Newly noticed phenomena may be analyzed, but they should be labeled exploratory for the dataset in which they were first noticed. Any later test can prospectively evaluate them with an appropriate prior-informed or replication label.

## Working principle

The operative question is not only:

> What did we see?

but also:

> What did we already know, expect, or have words for when we first saw it?

Naming that lens is part of the provenance of the result.
